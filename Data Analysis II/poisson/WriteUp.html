Poisson Regression Article

1 Background

1.1 Understanding the Technique

1.1.1 The Poisson Distribution

Poisson regression extends the linear least-squares regression model to poisson distributed data. Often used to represent physical events occurring, the poisson distribution measures the probability of \(X\) events occuring over a given interval of time, and the average rate of those events occuring is \(\lambda\). For example, the probability of \(X = 6\) hurricanes happening over a year, when the historic average is \(\lambda = \frac{4\, \text{hurricanes}}{\text{year}}\).

The poisson distribution can be viewed as a special case of the binomial distribution, which models the number of successes in a fixed number of trials (Oxford College of Emory University, Math Center, n.d.):

\[\begin{align*} P(X=x) = f_x(x) = {}_{n}C_xp^xq^{n-x} \end{align*}\]

Where \(n\) is the number of trials, and \(x\) is the number of successes.

For the binomial distribution, the mean/expected value is \(np\), which we’ll call \(\lambda\). So,

\[\begin{align*} p = \frac{\lambda}{n} \end{align*}\]

If \(q\) is the probability of a failure, \(q = 1-p\), or

\[\begin{align*} q = 1 - \frac{\lambda}{n} \end{align*}\]

Plugging back into our original equation:

\[\begin{align*} P(X = x) &= f_x(x) = {}_nC_x(\frac{\lambda}{n})^x(1-\frac{\lambda}{n})^{n-x} \\ &= \frac{n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n - x + 1)}{x!}\cdot \frac{\lambda^x}{n^x}(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n})^{n-x} \\ &= \frac{n}{n}\cdot\frac{n-1}{n}\cdots \frac{n-x+1}{n}\cdot \frac{\lambda^x}{x!}(1-\frac{\lambda}{n})^{n-x} \\ &= \frac{n}{n}\cdot \frac{n-1}{n}\cdots \frac{n-x+1}{n}\cdot \frac{\lambda^x}{x!}(1-\frac{\lambda}{n})^n(1-\frac{\lambda}{n})^{-x} \end{align*}\]

Note that the second to last expression is an expression for \(e^{-\lambda}\).

If we take the limit as our number of trials \(n\) approaches infinity while keeping \(x\) and \(\lambda\) fixed, all the terms except the second and third to last trend approach 1:

\[\begin{align*} \lim_{n \to \infty}P(X=x) = f_x(x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!} \end{align*}\]

This is the exact probability of \(x\) events occuring over \(\lambda\) time.

We can plot the probability mass function (PMF) to see the probability of \(x\) events occuring for every \(X\), noting how different \(\lambda\) values change that distribution:

remark: notice how as \(\lambda\) increases the distribution is more bell curved shaped.

The poisson distribution is particularly well behaved because of it’s distributions properties. It’s first two moments are given (Soch and The Book of Statistical Proofs contributors 2020):

Expected Value / Mean:

\[\begin{align*} E(x) =& \sum_{x\in \mathcal{X}}^{} x \cdot f_X(x) \\ =& \sum_{x = 0}^{\infty} x \cdot \frac{\lambda^xe^{-\lambda}}{x!} \\ =& \sum_{x = 1}^{\infty} x \cdot \frac{\lambda^xe^{-\lambda}}{x!} \\ = & e^{-\lambda} \cdot \sum_{x = 1}^{\infty} \frac{x}{x!}\lambda^x \\ = & \lambda e^{-\lambda} \cdot \sum_{x = 1}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^{x-1}}{(x-1)!} \\ \end{align*}\]

Let \(z = x-1 \implies x = z+1\):

\[\begin{align*} E(X) =& \lambda e^{-\lambda} \cdot \sum_{z = 0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^z}{z!} \end{align*}\]

Remember that the power series expansion of \(e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}\).

Finally, \[\begin{align*} E(X) =& \lambda e^{-\lambda} \cdot e^{\lambda} \\ =& \lambda \end{align*}\]

Variance:

\[\begin{align*} Var(X) =& E(X^2) - E(X)^2 \end{align*}\]

If we look at the expectation of \(X(X-1)\):

\[\begin{align*} E[X(X-1)] &= \sum_{x\in \mathcal{X}} x (x-1) \cdot f_X(x) \\ &= \sum_{x=0}^{\infty}x (x-1) \cdot \frac{\lambda^xe^{-\lambda}}{x!} \\ &= \sum_{x=2}^{\infty}x(x-1)\cdot \frac{\lambda^xe^{-\lambda}}{x!} \\ &= e^{-\lambda}\cdot \sum_{x=2}^{\infty}x(x-1)\cdot \frac{\lambda^x}{x\cdot(x-1)\cdot(x-2)!} \\ &= \lambda^2 \cdot e^{-\lambda}\cdot \sum_{x=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{x-2}}{(x-2)!} \cdot \end{align*}\]

let \(z=x-2 \implies x=z+2\), hence: \[\begin{align*} E[X(X-1)] &= \lambda^2\cdot e^{-\lambda} \cdot \sum_{z=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^z}{z!} \end{align*}\]

recall the power series expansion of \(e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}\):

\[\begin{align*} E[X(X-1)] = \lambda^2 \cdot e^{-\lambda} \cdot e^{\lambda} = \lambda^2 \end{align*}\]

Since, \[\begin{align*} E[X(X-1)] = E(X^2-X) = E(X^2) - E(X) \end{align*}\] now: \[\begin{align*} E(X^2) - E(X) = \lambda^2 \implies E(X^2) = \lambda^2 + \lambda \end{align*}\]

Finally, by plugging in: \[\begin{align*} Var(X) =& E(X^2) - E(X)^2 \\ =& \lambda^2 + \lambda - \lambda^2 \\ =& \lambda \end{align*}\]

We can verify this numerically. Here I simulate 10,000 poisson distributed datasets with \(\lambda = 1\) and sample size \(n=25\):

Code 
set.seed(13)
sims = 10000 #Simulations
lambda = 1  #Frequency Parameter
n = 25      #Sample Size

means <- rep(NA, sims) #Place to store means
vars <- rep(NA, sims)  #Place to store variances

for(i in 1:sims){
  poisson_dat <- rpois(n, lambda) #Simulate random data
  means[i] <- mean(poisson_dat)   #Store the mean of the data
  vars[i] <- var(poisson_dat)     #Store the variance of the data
}

The means and variances should both be centered around 1:

Even with some outliers here and there, it checks out.

1.1.2 Poisson Regression

Where the typical linear regression goes as follows: \[\begin{align*} Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \beta_2 x_{2i} + \cdots + \beta_{p-1} x_{(p-1)i} + \epsilon_i \end{align*}\]

Since \(Y_i\) is assumed to be continuous, both sides of the equation can range from \((-\infty, \infty)\). To model a variable that is based on counts, and therefore strictly \(>0\), we have to transform the data to sit in the \((-\infty, \infty)\) range, and this can be achieved with a log transformation:

\[\begin{align*} log(\lambda_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \cdots + \beta_{p-1} x_{(p-1)i} \end{align*}\] where \(\lambda_i\) is the parameter for the Poisson distribution from which the \(i\)th response is drawn.

If all of the data is not measured over the same unit of time, an offset variable is added to the equation: \[\begin{align*} \log(\lambda_i) = \left(\log(T_i) + \beta_0\right) + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \cdots + \beta_{p-1} x_{(p-1)i} \end{align*}\]

Lastly, a critical assumption of poisson regression is that the data behaves like it’s poisson distributed, with equal mean and variance (Figure 2). Otherwise, another type of regression (negative binomial, quasi-poisson, etc.) should be used.

1.2 The Study

Jie et al. look to further investigate the Janzen-Connell effect, which looks to explain the mechanism behind biodiversity, or why a single tree or plant species doesn’t just dominate a forest. In order for a plant to survive, it needs to not be eaten or destroyed by natural enemies (insects, animals, fungi, etc.). A common explanation behind biodiversity says that natural enemies specialize in eating/killing a specific plant over time, and those natural enemies accumulate in areas where there’s high densities of those specific plants. Because of this, a new seedling might actually have an easier time in an area without a lot of members of its own species because those natural enemies have not accumulated so heavily. Essentially, there’s always a push-and-pull between the plants and enemies in an area, and they balance each other out. (Jia et al. 2020).

For one of their analyses, the researchers here look to test factors that can affect a seedlings ability to survive around a lot of members of its own species (a.k.a recruitment):

  • How does the density of a plant’s own species affect its seedlings’ recruitment?
  • Which natural enemies drive seedling recruitment?

To control which natural enemies are present, a fence was used to keep out herbivores, and insecticides/fungicides were used to discourage insects/fungus.

To set up the experiment the researchers took 3 plots from a forest in China and divided them up into subplots:

Within each plot, half was fenced, and within each half, 10 squares were control, 10 had insecticide, and 10 had fungicide.

1.3 Goal of Statistical Analysis

For their regression equation, they regress seedling recruitment with fixed factors:

  • Pesticide treatment
  • Fencing exclosure
  • Density of adult plants (same species)
  • Density of adult plants (different species)
  • Plot
  • Census Date

Including a three-way interaction and pairwise two-way interactions between the first three.

And random factors:

  • Subplot (random intercept)
  • Species (random intercept)
  • Effect of census on subplots (random effect)

Also, same species density has been log transformed and Z-scored

In R, our formula call looks like this:

Code 
formula <- recruits ~ pesticide*exclosure*con_crowding + het_crowding + plot + census  + (1| subplot) + (1| species) + (0+census | subplot)

2.7 Conclusions

If we harken back to the study background, the Janzen-Connell effect tells us that we should expect new seedlings to struggle propagating in an area that is already densely populated with its own species, as there is a higher chance that natural enemies have begun to accumulate there. What we found through our data doesn’t necessarily support this in every case however. Looking at the global trend of same species density, seedlings tends to be better off when there is a higher density of their own species (Figure 12), though this is only true for groups with insecticide and fungicide (Table 10). The trend for the control group isn’t statistically discernible from zero, so we can’t say for sure which direction this trend goes (Table 10). However, if we look at the hypothesis generously, we might say that the groups being treated with pesticides, especially fungicide (Table 11) would have fewer natural enemies to fight off, and in that case a higher same species density would likely just indicate that that species happens to do well there, and it makes sense for more seedlings to appear. Lastly when discussing the same species trend, the three-way interaction between exclosure type, pesticide, and same species density did not indicate that any of those trends differed by whether they were enclosed or not (Table 4).

When investigating which natural enemies have the strongest effect on seedling recruitment, the global trend indicates that the fungicide had the most noticeable effect on recruitment, being noticeably greater than the control and insecticide groups (Table 8). Across exclosure types, the effect of pesticides and being in the control groups shifted heavily (Table 9). While little change was noticed in the fungicide group, being fenced in helped the insecticide group but hurt the control group (Figure 15). The fungicide group was only better off compared to the insecticide group when the exclosure was open, but that effect reversed compared to the control when the exclosure was fenced (Table 9). For the global effect of exclosure type, there was no statistically discernible difference between the open and fenced groups (Table 7).

3 Disagreements With the Article

3.1 Results

3.1.1 Pesticide and Same Species Density Interaction

For the interaction between fungicide and same species density, I agree partially with the article when they say:

“Protection from fungi increased … recruitment … of seedlings when conspecific adult densities were high (Fungicide × conspecific adult density interactions, recruitment: Poisson GLMM, \(z = 4.87, P < 0.001\)…)”

The interaction in my model summary wasn’t quite as strong as there’s though I still found it statistically discernible (Table 4). Their wording makes me think they looked at the effect of the pesticides and varying levels of same species density, which I reversed, looking at the effect of same species density on the pesticides. Still, I’m assuming this value comes from their model summary, which should be the same as mine. Also, I’ve only heard of staggering levels of a continuous variable in an estimated marginal means/trends setting, not for a standard GLMM summary, but they don’t mention either technique in the manuscript or supplemental materials.

For the interaction between insecticide and same species density, I don’t get the same result as the article when they say:

“Suppression of insects also increased seedling recruitment at high conspecific adult densities (Poisson GLMM: Insecticide × conspecific adult density interaction, \(z = 2.19, P = 0.029\))…”

In my model summary, the interaction between same species density and the insecticide treatment was not statistically discernible from zero, and it wasn’t particularly close (Table 4).

If we compare their figure looking at the interaction between same species density and pesticide to mine (Figure 16):

So while the authors use the GLMM predictions and each dot is the mean and SE of the observed values at that density level, I opted for the estimated marginal trends, as I think that does a nicer job of untangling those interactions while making the trends visually understandable. Their plot also has a much narrower range of x values compared to mine, so you’re seeing a much more truncated effect. I notice that their trends for the insecticide and fungicide groups have a logarithmic shape, whereas mine look more linear and quadratic for both groups respectively.

3.1.2 Effect of Fencing in Plots

For the effect of fencing in the plots, I come to a similar conclusion but obtained different numbers:

“The effect of large vertebrates was not estimated to be large in any of the models (Recruitment: Poisson GLMM, \(z = −0.23, P = 818\)…)”

I’m assuming that \(P = 818\) is a typo. Either way, in my model summary, the coefficient estimate for fencing in the enclosures was marginally discernible, with very different statistics (Table 4). If we assume they’re talking about the estimated marginal means effect that isolates that variable, my methods also found a non-discernible effect of the enclosures, but with different statistics once again (Table 7).

3.2 Discussion

The first sentence of the discussion reads:

“The deleterious effects of natural enemies on seedling recruitment and survival, assessed at the community scale, increase with the local density of conspecific adults.”

I’d say this is only true for fungicide. Insecticide is not discernibly better than the control for seedlings at any level of same species density (Table 7). They seem to change their wording slightly later in the paragraph, so I’m not sure why they it isn’t consistent with the first sentence:

“Thus, our results suggest that plant-associated fungi suppress the recruitment of tree species dominating the nearby canopy, which will increase turnover of tree species and could increase diversity.”

Once again, they repeat:

“While the evidence that natural enemies reduce seedling performance at high conspecific density in our system is in line with previous pesticide application experiments…”

This is only true of the fungicide group. I also tested the effect of the exclosure at different levels of same species density:

As we can see, the effect of exclosure type, ostensibly meant to correspond with natural herbivores, is not discernible from zero at any level of same species density (Table 13).

They repeat the same claim I think is too broad later on as well:

“Instead, our results suggest that density of adult conspecifics is a better predictor of natural enemy impacts on recruitment and survival of seedlings.”

3.2.1 Effect of Same Species Density on Unmanipulated Plots

Later in the discussion, they make the claim:

“These natural enemy impacts appear to be important, even though the net effect of conspecific density is only weakly negative in plots when natural enemies are unmanipulated.”

When the authors claim,

This is simply not true for seedling recruitment. None of the groups across pesticide and exclosure types had a statistically discernible negative trend of same species density on seedling recruitment(Table 14).

Later on the authors again claim:

“…on the basis of the evidence presented here, insects and plant-associated fungi appear to play a greater role in mediating conspecific density dependence than large herbivores.”

I don’t see how they can claim this when the effect of insecticide on seedling recruitment is not discernible at any level of same species density (Table 12).

3.2.2 Diagnosing the Differences

To try and understand how these differences arose, I checked to see if their were any differences in our code, and I think I understand some of them. As for the first difference in the interaction between fungicide and same species density, the authors of the paper changed the glm() formula once they found out that the three-way interaction between pesticide, exclosure, and same species density was not significant. After changing the model to only a two-way interaction between pesticide and same species density, that interaction between fungicide and same species density was a lot stronger.

These kind of post-hoc statistical adjustments, I believe, are very antithetical to what statisticians are telling us about responsible research ethics. There’s a reason that high standard journals, like Nature, are starting to require pre-approved methods before running any analysis on an experiment. Picking and choosing variables like this by testing different models is certain to bias the experiment. At least with an algorithmic selection approach like Lasso, the decisions are not being made subjectively by humans. I would at least expect the researchers to provide \(p\)-value adjustments to their coefficients given they’ve created several models. Not as important, but also frustrating is the fact that their code doesn’t run cleanly from start to finish, and different scripts require objects defined in the other (or not at all), indicating that nobody thought to check that they run on a new session in R.
